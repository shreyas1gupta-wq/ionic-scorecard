A few months ago I started building a company inside a company. No job postings, no interviews, no payroll — just roles I kept wishing existed on a research desk, added one at a time, until they added up to something that runs like one. One role's entire job is trying to kill my own results before I'm allowed to trust them. Another exists purely to catch the specific ways a backtest quietly lies — a timestamp shifted a day into the future, a "clean" universe that's forgotten every stock that ever got delisted, an edge that only survives because tomorrow's price leaked into yesterday's decision. Nothing is allowed to look real until something has tried to make it fall apart by shuffling the dates and watching whether it collapses.

None of them are people — they're roles, running as an agentic system. Here's the opinion that's earned itself the hard way: AI is still genuinely bad at telling me what to test. Every hypothesis on the board started as mine. What it's extraordinary at is everything after that — running the backtest, hunting the edge case, checking if a result survives being shuffled, freezing a strategy's code the moment it goes to paper so I can't quietly tune it mid-test and fool myself later.

Where the seams show: it won't hand me a genuinely new idea. It can't yet tell "this data source looks a little off" from "this data source is lying to me" without me looking first. And the one gate that never auto-advances — the one that would commit real capital — stays mine, on purpose, by design.

So it's not "AI replaced a research team." It's a system built to be paranoid by default, paired with a person who still supplies the two things it can't: the question worth asking, and the final call. That combination compounds faster than either half alone.
