## Draft text (LinkedIn)

The most expensive mistake in research isn't a wrong answer. It's a confident answer to a question whose premise was already broken.

You can feel the difference. A wrong answer gets caught — the number looks off, a check fails, someone argues with you. A broken premise doesn't get caught, because everything downstream of it is internally consistent. The math is clean. The chart is beautiful. The backtest is spectacular. And none of it means anything, because the thing you measured wasn't the thing you thought you were measuring.

I've learned to distrust results that arrive too clean. When a model looks brilliant, my first question now isn't "is the calculation right." It's "what did I assume to even ask this?" More often than I'd like, the edge was hiding in an assumption I never wrote down: a timestamp that quietly shifted a day, a price that wasn't the price I thought, a filter that leaked the future into the past.
